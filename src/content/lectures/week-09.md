Week 1 showed OnTheList's box: a perforated tear-strip and a printed
Aviation Security label. That label is the visible tip of a paperwork
requirement most of this course has left unexamined. This week is the
paperwork.

## What the label is standing in for

A parcel containing a flammable liquid — a fragrance, in OnTheList's case —
can't just be labelled and shipped. Per IATA and carrier requirements
(UPS's shipper responsibilities page states the same requirement other
carriers apply), the shipper must complete a dangerous goods declaration
naming: the substance's UN/ID number, its proper shipping name, its hazard
class, its packing group, and the specific IATA packing instruction number
used to pack it for that mode of transport. Three original copies of the
declaration are required per hazardous package — not photocopies, not a
single form covering a batch, three originals per package.

None of this is the tear-strip's job. The tear-strip (easy, tool-free
opening for a courier or customs handler) and the printed label (visible
warning to anyone handling the box) are the *physical* half of compliance.
The declaration paperwork is the *administrative* half, and it's the half
that actually determines whether a carrier will put the parcel on a plane
at all — a perfect label on a package with a missing or incorrect
declaration doesn't fly, in either sense.

## Whose rule this actually is

None of these requirements come from OnTheList, from the fragrance's
manufacturer, or from a sustainability standard. They come from IATA (the
International Air Transport Association), enforced at the carrier level —
a rule written by an aviation safety body managing cargo-hold risk, not by
anyone with a stake in how a customer feels opening the box. This is
compliance logic in its purest form in this course: the packaging design
choices (tear-strip, label placement) are constrained by a requirement
neither the brand nor the buyer had any say in writing, and the cost of
getting it wrong isn't a bad review — it's a refused shipment, a fine, or
worse.
