## The claim

Sustainability language on packaging is mostly marketing. What actually
decides how a package is built is a shipping-cost formula and a
return/liability calculation. This isn't a cynical aside — it's the thing
this course spends twelve weeks trying to break, or fail to break.

Three packages make the case before any formula does.

## Three boxes, three logics

**Apple.** Lift the lid and the product sits under a layer of its own,
accessories tucked behind a hidden second panel you find by touch, not by
looking. Every material and colour choice agrees with every other one. None
of this is required to get a phone from a warehouse to a doorstep intact —
it is required to make opening the box feel like part of buying the phone.
Call this **brand-experience logic**: the box is doing marketing work, and
its cost is justified as marketing spend, not as shipping spend.

**lululemon, Patagonia.** A pair of shoes, or a small backpack, arrives in a
box several times the size of the thing inside. Nobody designed this box to
look generous. A retailer that ships thousands of SKUs a day gets a real
cost benefit from stocking three or four standard box sizes instead of one
custom size per product — less warehouse decision-making, faster packing,
fewer SKU-specific tooling costs — and eats the extra cardboard and the
extra air freight as the price of that standardisation. Call this
**logistics-cost logic**: the box is sized for the warehouse's convenience,
not the product's.

**OnTheList (an Australian online fragrance and skincare marketplace).** The
box has a perforated tear-strip moulded into one side, so it opens without a
knife, and a printed "Aviation Security" dangerous-goods declaration label,
because the contents are flammable liquids and the carrier is required to
know that before it puts the box on a plane. Neither feature is about the
customer's experience or the warehouse's shelving. Both exist because a
regulator or a fulfilment contract requires them. Call this
**compliance logic**: the box is built to satisfy a rule, and the rule was
written by someone other than the brand or the buyer.

## What none of the three logics needed

None of these three explanations required an appeal to the environment.
Apple's reveal is about desire. Lululemon and Patagonia's oversized box is
about warehouse cost. OnTheList's tear-strip and label are about aviation
regulation. If a box also happens to carry a recyclability claim, that claim
sits on top of one of these three logics — it doesn't replace any of them,
and by the throughline claim above, it usually isn't the thing actually
driving the design.

## Where the rest of the course goes

Each logic above is a description, not a measurement, and a description is
easy to argue with. The next eleven weeks put numbers behind it:

- **Week 2** asks what the box is actually made of, and what that costs.
- **Week 3** asks what "surviving shipping" is actually tested against, and
  who decided the pass mark.
- **Week 4** turns "the box is oversized" into an actual formula a carrier
  bills against.
- **Week 5** turns to the sustainability claims themselves, and asks who
  publishes them and what they have to gain by publishing them.

By the midterm you should be able to look at an arbitrary package and name,
with a number attached, which of these logics is actually paying for it.
