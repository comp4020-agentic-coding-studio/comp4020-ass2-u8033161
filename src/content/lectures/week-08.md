So far every logic in this course has been a cost or a compliance
argument. This week is the exception: brand-experience logic, revisited
through the psychology it's actually built on, rather than through a
formula.

## Choice architecture, briefly

Thaler and Sunstein's *Nudge* (2008) describes **choice architecture**: the
way any environment that presents options — a cafeteria layout, a default
checkbox, a form's ordering — shapes the choice made, without removing any
option or changing its price. A "nudge" doesn't ban or subsidise anything;
it changes what's default, what's salient, or how something is framed, and
people's choices move anyway. The book's own examples are mostly about
health and finance defaults, not packaging — this week is the transfer of
that framework to a domain the authors weren't writing about.

## Unboxing as choice architecture

A "reveal" sequence — lift the lid, find a layer, uncover a hidden panel —
is choice architecture applied to a moment that has no real choice left in
it: the purchase is already made. What it can still shape is **perceived
value** and **attention**. Week 1's Apple case is the clearest example: the
product doesn't sit loose in the box, it sits under a first layer that has
to be lifted, with accessories placed where you find them by touch rather
than sight. Nothing about the phone changes. What's engineered is the
sequence in which information becomes available — a form of salience and
framing, not persuasion about a decision still to be made.

OnTheList's tear-strip (week 1, revisited properly in week 9) can be read
the same way for a different purpose: making the "correct" way to open the
box — the way that doesn't require a knife near a flammable-liquid parcel —
the path of least resistance, which is closer to Thaler and Sunstein's
original safety-default examples than to Apple's marketing reveal. Same
framework, two different goals: one shapes perceived value, the other
shapes safe behaviour.

## What this doesn't explain

Nudge theory explains why a reveal sequence might change how a product
feels to receive. It says nothing about whether that reveal is worth its
cost in material, box complexity or assembly time — that's still week 2's
and week 4's argument, not this week's. A well-designed reveal and an
expensive one aren't the same claim, and this course's throughline claim
still asks which cost is actually paying for a given design choice. This
week adds a *why it works on a person* answer to logics that, until now,
have mostly explained *why it's cheap or required*.
