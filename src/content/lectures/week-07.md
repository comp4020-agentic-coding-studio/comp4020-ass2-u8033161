Every week so far has priced the outbound half of a shipment: the box, the
drop test, the freight formula. Half of the throughline claim's cost side
has been missing. This week fills it in: what happens, and what it costs,
when the product comes back.

## The numbers

Roughly 19.3% of e-commerce orders are returned overall — but that average
hides a wide spread by category: apparel 20-40%, footwear 17-30%,
electronics 8-15%, beauty 4-12%, jewelry 12-15%. Processing a return isn't
free: reverse-logistics and restocking for electronics runs about $30-65
per item, and for furniture or other oversized goods, the reverse-logistics
cost can exceed the entire margin on the item. About half of all apparel
returns come down to fit and sizing alone, not a defect or a change of
mind. Some brands now issue a "keep-it" refund — refunding the customer
without requiring the item back — once the cost of collecting, inspecting
and restocking it would exceed what the item is worth.

## What this does to week 1's lululemon case

Apparel sits at the top of the return-rate table, and half of that is fit
alone — something no amount of packaging can fix, since the product itself
is the variable. But packaging still touches this cost in two ways.
First, generous sizing options mean more units in circulation that are
statistically likely to come back, which is itself a reason to keep the
outbound packaging cheap and standardised (week 4's oversized-box logic)
rather than premium — a returned item that gets reshipped in the same
generic box costs less to process than one that needed custom packaging
undone and redone. Second, an easy-to-repack box lowers the labour cost of
that return cycle, which is a real, if less visible, line item next to the
freight cost week 4 already put a number on.

The "keep-it" refund is the sharpest version of this logic: once you've
built a return-cost model like the one above, refusing the item back and
eating the loss on the product is sometimes cheaper than collecting it. This
isn't generosity. It's the same cost-minimising logic as every other
decision in this course, applied to the one direction of shipping we hadn't
priced yet.
