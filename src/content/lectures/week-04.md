Week 2 costed a box by its material. Week 3 costed it by what it has to
survive. Neither of those numbers is what actually appears on a shipping
invoice. This week is the formula that does: Australia Post's cubic weight
calculation, which is close to what every major carrier runs in some form.

## The formula

> Cubic weight (kg) = length (m) x width (m) x height (m) x 250

A courier doesn't just weigh your parcel — it also converts the parcel's
outer dimensions into a "cubic weight" using the formula above, then bills
against **whichever number is bigger**, the cubic weight or the actual
weight. This comparison typically kicks in once a parcel's actual weight
passes about 1kg; below that, small parcels are usually billed on actual
weight regardless of size. The 250 constant isn't arbitrary physics — it's
a density assumption the carrier has decided is "normal" for a parcel of a
given volume, and anything less dense than that assumption gets billed as
if it weren't.

## A worked example (illustrative dimensions, not a measured parcel)

To see how sharply this bites, work through a hypothetical parcel — the
dimensions and weight below are invented for this exercise, not a real
quoted or measured figure:

A pair of shoes and their retail box weigh **1.2kg actual**, and ship
inside an outer box measuring **0.40m x 0.35m x 0.25m**. Its cubic weight
is:

> 0.40 x 0.35 x 0.25 x 250 = **8.75kg**

Chargeable weight is the greater of the two: **8.75kg**, not 1.2kg. The
courier bills this parcel as if it weighs more than seven times what it
actually does — and the entire difference is air, created by the box's
outer dimensions rather than the product inside it.

## What this does to week 1's oversized-box case

This is the number behind week 1's lululemon and Patagonia case: an
oversized standard box that saves the warehouse a packing decision doesn't
just cost more cardboard (week 2) — every centimetre of unnecessary length,
width or height gets multiplied straight into the freight bill, for every
unit shipped, forever. A retailer running this formula against their own
box sizes can put an exact dollar figure on "how much does standardising
box sizes cost us in freight," and weigh it against the labour and tooling
savings on the other side of that trade. That's not a guess anymore — it's
the calculation logistics-cost logic is actually running.
