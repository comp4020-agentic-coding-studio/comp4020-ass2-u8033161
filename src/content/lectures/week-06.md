No new source this week. This lecture spends its time putting weeks 2
through 5 back onto the three week 1 case studies, because a framework you
can only apply to the case it was invented for isn't a framework yet.

## Apple

Week 2 suggested Apple's box likely leans toward a thin, print-friendly
board (E-flute or similar) rather than a thick cushioning flute — brand
experience doesn't need heavy impact protection for a phone travelling in
its own rigid inner tray. Week 4's cubic-weight formula mostly works in
Apple's favour here: a dense, tightly-fitted box for a small heavy object
rarely trips the cubic-weight penalty the way an oversized box does. Week 5
asks whether Apple makes a sustainability claim on this packaging at all,
and if so, whether it's closer to a genuine material-reduction effort or a
claim layered on top of a design that was never primarily about the
environment. The diagnosis: Apple is optimising for perceived value at the
moment of opening, and is willing to pay a real premium — in material
choice, in fitted-tray tooling cost — to protect that experience, because
the box's job is to be marketing collateral that happens to also ship a
phone.

## lululemon, Patagonia

This is the case week 4 was built for. An oversized standard box scores
badly on the cubic-weight formula — every centimetre of unused space gets
billed as if it were product. That looks like waste until you put week 2's
board-cost logic next to it: a warehouse running three or four standard box
sizes instead of one custom size per SKU saves real money on packing
labour, tooling and shelving decisions, at the cost of that cubic-weight
freight penalty. This is a genuine trade-off, not simply carelessness — the
retailer has decided warehouse efficiency is worth more than the freight
premium, at whatever volume they ship. The diagnosis: this is logistics-cost
logic in the strict sense — not "the cheapest box," but the box that's
cheapest once you account for the whole fulfilment operation, not just the
one parcel in front of you.

## OnTheList

Week 3's ISTA 3A leak-test requirement and week 9's dangerous-goods
paperwork (coming later this semester) both sit underneath this case's
tear-strip and Aviation Security label. Neither feature is optimising for
cost in the way lululemon's box is, or for brand experience in the way
Apple's is — a compliance failure here isn't a bad review, it's a shipment
a carrier refuses to fly. The diagnosis: OnTheList is optimising for
regulatory and carrier acceptance first, with cost and brand experience
constrained to whatever's left over once the compliance requirements are
met.

## The pattern across all three

None of the three cases needed an environmental explanation to make sense,
and none of the numbers gathered since week 1 have changed that. What's
changed is that "brand experience," "logistics cost," and "compliance" are
no longer just plausible descriptions — each is now backed by a specific
formula or standard you could run yourself on an arbitrary fourth package.
That's the test the second half of the course applies: not new logics, but
harder, more entangled cases where more than one logic pulls in different
directions at once.
