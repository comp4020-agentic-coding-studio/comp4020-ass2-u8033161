No new source this week. Week 1 opened with three packages and a claim
this course would spend twelve weeks testing. This lecture is short on
purpose — the real work this week is in the defense, not in more content.

## Where the claim stands

Across eleven weeks: a flute choice is a cost-and-protection trade with no
sustainability content of its own (week 2). A drop-test standard prices out
return and liability risk, not environmental harm (week 3). An oversized
box is a real, quantifiable freight cost, chosen because it's cheaper for
the warehouse, not because anyone thinks it's fine to ship air (week 4).
Even a rigorous, peer-reviewed sustainability data source turned out to be
published by the industry it favours (week 5). Returns cost real money to
process, and some brands would rather refund an item than get it back
(week 7). A reveal sequence works on a customer psychologically, separate
from what it costs to build (week 8). A dangerous-goods label is standing
in for paperwork written by a regulator with no interest in the customer's
experience (week 9). And accessible design is a constraint none of the
other logics were built to notice at all (week 10).

None of the eleven weeks needed to invoke "sustainability" as the real
driver of a design decision. That's the throughline claim holding up
across eleven applications — not proof it's universally true, but eleven
cases where a plausible-sounding environmental story turned out to have a
cheaper, more specific explanation underneath it.

## What week 11 asks of that claim

Your own redesign is the twelfth test, and the first one you built rather
than analysed. Locate it: which of week 1's three logics — brand
experience, logistics cost, compliance — does your redesign lean on most?
Where does it complicate the throughline claim rather than confirm it —
did you make a genuinely sustainability-motivated choice anywhere, and if
so, what did it actually cost, and was that cost worth it on its own
terms rather than as marketing?
