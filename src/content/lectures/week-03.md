Week 2 gave flute choice a cost side. This week gives it a protection side:
what does a box actually have to survive, and who decided the pass mark?

## What ISTA 3A actually checks

ISTA 3A (International Safe Transit Association, general simulation
standard) applies to packaged products up to 70kg, across four package
shapes — standard, small, flat, and elongated. The full procedure:

1. **Atmospheric preconditioning** — the package sits at a controlled
   temperature and humidity before testing, since cardboard's strength
   changes with moisture.
2. **9 drops, then 8 more drops** — on different faces, edges and corners,
   simulating the repeated handling a box actually gets between a warehouse
   and a doorstep.
3. **Random vibration**, with and without a top load — simulating a truck
   ride, both alone in a van and stacked under other freight.
4. **Optional low-pressure vibration** — for anything that might fly.
5. **Rotational edge and flat drops, concentrated and bridge impact tests**
   — corner- and edge-first impacts a straight drop test misses.
6. **A leak test** for anything liquid — directly relevant to week 1's
   OnTheList case.

## Why this is a cost story, not an environmental one

Nobody runs this test out of concern for the product. A company runs it
because a package that fails in transit becomes a return, a refund, a
replacement shipment, a one-star review, or — for a liquid that leaks in an
aircraft hold — a regulatory incident. ISTA 3A is how a company prices out
the return/liability half of the throughline claim *before* it ships a
million units and finds out the expensive way.

This also complicates week 2's flute story rather than just extending it. A
box isn't over- or under-specced against some abstract "how tough is
this product" scale — it's specced against whichever ISTA test elements the
company actually runs, or skips. A retailer that skips vibration testing
because their product line rarely gets stacked is making the same
cost-trade-off logic as a retailer who runs the full suite for a fragile,
high-return-cost item. Neither is greener or less green than the other; both
are pricing the same risk differently.
